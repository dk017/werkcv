# Your CV Is Making the Recruiter Do Unpaid Work

*Why qualified international candidates disappear in Dutch hiring, and how to remove the uncertainty that causes it*

There is a kind of CV that is difficult to reject and difficult to accept.

The candidate is capable. The companies are real. The degree looks serious. The experience may even be unusually strong.

But the document leaves work for the reader.

What role does this person want? Is "Senior Executive" a leader or a coordinator? Does "good Dutch" mean they can greet a colleague or handle a customer call? Are they already in the Netherlands? Can they work here? Did they improve anything, or were they simply present while the work happened?

None of these questions is fatal. Together, they create friction. The recruiter stops evaluating the candidate and starts interpreting the document.

That is unpaid work.

Most CV advice focuses on making candidates sound more impressive. A better objective is to make them easier to understand.

This matters in a Dutch labour market that remains active but has become less forgiving. Statistics Netherlands reported about 378,000 vacancies at the end of the first quarter of 2026, with 91 vacancies for every 100 unemployed people. Employers still need talent, but the market is less tight than it was at its 2022 peak. Ambiguity has become more expensive. ([CBS](https://www.cbs.nl/en-gb/visualisations/labour-market-dashboard))

The central question for an international applicant is not, "How do I make my CV look Dutch?"

It is, "How do I stop making a Dutch employer guess?"

## A CV is a compression problem

Your career contains too much information. A CV compresses years of work into one or two pages without losing the facts that matter.

International candidates face an extra problem: their career was encoded in one system and must be read in another. Job titles do not travel cleanly. Degrees do not map perfectly. A famous employer at home may be unknown abroad.

Simply translating the words is not enough. You must translate the meaning.

Official Dutch guidance says a CV should show who you are, what you can do, where you worked and what education you completed. It should also be clear enough for someone to understand your experience quickly. ([Work in NL](https://www.workinnl.nl/en/employment/cv-en/default.aspx))

Here are seven questions your CV should answer without asking the recruiter to investigate.

## 1. What job are you applying for?

Candidates often put their current title under their name. That helps only when the current title and target role are the same.

Consider:

> Operations Executive

That could describe an entry-level coordinator, an experienced specialist or a director. The recruiter should not have to read page two to find out.

Use the language of the vacancy:

> Supply Chain Coordinator  
> SaaS Customer Support Specialist  
> Financial Controller  
> Backend Software Engineer

Then write a short profile answering four things: what you do, how much relevant experience you have, what evidence makes you credible and what role you want.

Compare:

> Motivated professional with excellent communication skills seeking a challenging opportunity.

with:

> Customer support specialist with four years of SaaS experience. Handles high-volume email and chat support in Zendesk, focusing on billing and account issues. Seeking an English-speaking support role in the Netherlands.

The first is positive but empty. The second lets the reader classify the candidate.

## 2. What did you change?

Responsibilities are safe to write:

> Responsible for customer communication.

> Worked on monthly reporting.

These describe the job, not the person. A useful bullet normally contains:

**Action + scope + outcome**

For example:

> Resolved 35-45 customer requests per day across email and chat, maintaining a 94% satisfaction score during the holiday peak.

> Automated the monthly sales report in Power BI, reducing preparation time from two days to three hours.

Not every job produces a percentage. Do not invent one. Scope is also evidence: team size, customer volume, budget, geography, systems, frequency or complexity.

The purpose of a number is not decoration. It gives the reader a scale.

## 3. Where are you, and when can you start?

International candidates sometimes omit location because they fear it will count against them. Silence creates a worse problem. The recruiter must guess whether the candidate is in Amsterdam, arriving next month or applying speculatively from another continent.

One line removes the uncertainty:

> Based in Utrecht; available from 1 August 2026.

> Relocating to Rotterdam in September 2026.

> Based in Berlin; open to relocation to the Netherlands.

You do not need a full street address. A city, region or relocation statement is enough.

## 4. Can you legally work in the Netherlands?

Answer this carefully, not dramatically.

EU, EEA and Swiss nationals can work in the Netherlands without a Dutch work permit. Many other candidates require an employer-supported route. For highly skilled migrants, only an employer recognised by the IND can apply, and salary and other conditions apply. ([IND](https://ind.nl/en/residence-permits/work/highly-skilled-migrant))

Your CV is not an immigration application. It does not need passport numbers or a legal essay. It may need one accurate sentence:

> Eligible to work in the Netherlands without sponsorship.

> Currently holds a Dutch orientation-year residence permit.

> Requires employer sponsorship for a highly skilled migrant permit.

Only state what is true. The purpose is not to make sponsorship disappear. It is to prevent the employer from discovering it after three interviews.

## 5. What can you actually do in Dutch?

"Basic Dutch" is almost meaningless. It could mean twenty words or a simple conversation.

Use a recognised level when possible. Europass uses the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages, from A1 to C2. ([Europass](https://europass.europa.eu/en/how-self-assess-your-language-skills))

For example:

> English: C1 professional  
> Dutch: A2, currently taking weekly lessons  
> German: B1 conversational

If spoken and written ability differ, say so:

> Dutch: B1 conversation, A2 writing

Do not translate your CV into Dutch merely to look local if you cannot discuss it in Dutch during the interview. When the vacancy and working language are English, a clear English CV is more credible than awkward Dutch.

Some jobs are available in English; learning Dutch expands the range of opportunities. Your CV should help the employer decide whether your current level matches this role. ([Work in NL](https://www.workinnl.nl/en/employment/finding-work/default.aspx))

## 6. What does your education mean here?

Foreign qualifications are often left unexplained:

> B.Tech (CSE)

Write the meaning plainly:

> Bachelor of Technology in Computer Science and Engineering (four-year degree)

But do not invent a Dutch equivalence. Nuffic and SBB provide official credential evaluations through IDW. Nuffic also makes clear that an evaluation is not legally binding; employers still decide whom to hire. ([Nuffic](https://www.nuffic.nl/en/study-and-work-in-the-netherlands/applying-for-a-credential-evaluation))

Most unregulated jobs do not require a formal evaluation. Regulated professions may require recognition or registration.

Translate the degree name into clear English, retain the institution and country, and mention a Dutch comparison only when you have a basis for it.

## 7. Can the document be read without effort?

Design is useful when it creates hierarchy. It is harmful when it competes with the content.

The reader should distinguish job title, employer, dates, location and achievements without learning your visual system. Parsing software also has limits. Greenhouse lists graphics, image-only resumes, tables, headers, footers, text boxes, columns and inconsistent sections among formats that can prevent correct importing. ([Greenhouse](https://support.greenhouse.io/hc/en-us/articles/200989175-Unsuccessful-resume-parse))

Use readable type, consistent dates, clear headings, reverse-chronological experience, real text and enough white space.

Avoid skill bars claiming you are "85% leadership." Avoid icons that replace words such as phone or email. Avoid layouts where dates, employers and descriptions no longer line up.

The best design does not make the CV look expensive. It makes the candidate easy to understand.

## The uncertainty test

Read only the first half of your first page. Then answer:

- What role is this person targeting?
- What are they demonstrably good at?
- Where are they based?
- What languages can they use at work?
- Is their work-authorisation situation clear when relevant?
- Can I understand their latest role without knowing another country's title system?
- Is there evidence, rather than a list of traits?

If the answers are missing, adding adjectives will not help.

Remove uncertainty first.

While building WerkCV, I turned this framework into a practical [Dutch CV checker for the Netherlands](https://werkcv.nl/en/dutch-cv-checker). It checks an existing CV for local structure, readability and missing context. But the underlying test needs no software: every sentence should help the reader classify you, trust you or understand your fit.

Everything else is taking up space.

## What a Dutch CV really means

There is no secret Dutch format that guarantees an interview.

A Dutch CV is simply a document adapted to a specific decision. The employer is deciding whether your experience is relevant, whether the practical conditions work and whether a conversation is worth the time.

International applicants lose when the necessary information is hidden behind unfamiliar titles, vague language, excessive detail or silence.

Your experience does not become less valuable when it crosses a border.

But its meaning can become less obvious.

The job of the CV is to restore that meaning.
