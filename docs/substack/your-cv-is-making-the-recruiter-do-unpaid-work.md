# Your CV Is Making the Recruiter Do Unpaid Work

There is a particular kind of CV that is difficult to reject and difficult to accept.

The candidate is clearly capable. The companies are real. The degree looks serious. The experience may even be unusually strong.

But the document leaves work for the reader.

What role does this person actually want?

Is "Senior Executive" a senior leadership position, or a job title used differently in another country?

Does "good Dutch" mean they can greet a colleague, handle a customer call, or write a contract?

Are they already in the Netherlands?

Can they work here?

Did they improve anything, or were they merely present while the work happened?

None of these questions is fatal by itself. Together, they create friction. The recruiter must stop evaluating the candidate and start interpreting the document.

That is unpaid work. Most CV advice focuses on making a candidate sound more impressive. A better objective is to make the candidate easier to understand.

This matters more now than it did a few years ago. The Dutch labour market remains active, but it is less forgiving than it was at its tightest. Statistics Netherlands reported 378,000 open vacancies at the end of the first quarter of 2026, with 91 vacancies for every 100 unemployed people. That is still a substantial market, but vacancies have been declining from their 2022 peak. Employers can be more selective, and ambiguity becomes more expensive. ([CBS](https://www.cbs.nl/en-gb/visualisations/labour-market-dashboard))

The central problem for an international applicant is therefore not: "How do I make my CV Dutch?"

It is: "How do I stop making a Dutch employer guess?"

## A CV is a compression problem

Your career contains too much information.

A CV is an attempt to compress ten or twenty years into one or two pages without losing the facts that matter. International candidates face an additional problem: their careers were encoded in one system and must be read in another.

Job titles do not travel cleanly. Degrees do not map perfectly. The status attached to a company name may disappear at the border. A phrase that signals seniority in one country may signal nothing in another.

This is why simply translating the words is not enough. You have to translate the meaning.

Official Dutch guidance describes a CV in straightforward terms: it should show who you are, what you can do, where you worked, and what education you completed. More importantly, it should be clear enough for the reader to understand your experience quickly. ([Work in NL](https://www.workinnl.nl/en/employment/cv-en/default.aspx))

"Quickly" is the important word. Not because every recruiter follows a mythical six-second rule, but because clarity is part of the evidence. If you cannot explain your own fit clearly, the reader has less reason to believe you will explain complex work clearly after being hired.

Here are the seven questions an international CV should answer without asking the recruiter to investigate.

## 1. What job are you applying for?

Many candidates put their current title under their name. That is useful only when the current title and the target role are the same.

Suppose your header says:

> Operations Executive

That title may describe an entry-level coordinator, an experienced specialist, or a company director. The recruiter should not have to discover which one by reading page two.

Write the role you are targeting in language the vacancy already uses:

> Supply Chain Coordinator  
> SaaS Customer Support Specialist  
> Financial Controller  
> Backend Software Engineer

This is not manipulation. It is classification.

Your short profile should then answer four things:

1. What kind of professional are you?
2. How much relevant experience do you have?
3. What evidence makes you credible?
4. What kind of role are you seeking in the Netherlands?

Compare:

> Motivated professional with excellent communication skills seeking a challenging opportunity in a dynamic company.

with:

> Customer support specialist with four years of experience in SaaS and e-commerce. Handles high-volume email and chat support in Zendesk, with a focus on billing, account access, and complaint resolution. Seeking an English-speaking support role in an international team in the Netherlands.

The first version is positive but empty. The second lets the reader classify the candidate.

## 2. What did you change?

Responsibilities are easy to write because they are safe.

> Responsible for customer communication.

> Worked on monthly reporting.

> Managed social media.

These statements describe the job, not the person. They tell the recruiter what someone in the role was supposed to do. They do not show what this candidate did particularly well.

A useful bullet usually contains three elements:

**Action + scope + outcome**

For example:

> Resolved 35-45 customer requests per day across email and chat, maintaining a 94% satisfaction score during the holiday peak.

> Automated the monthly sales report in Power BI, reducing preparation time from two days to three hours for a 12-person account team.

> Planned and published LinkedIn content for three B2B product lines, increasing qualified demo enquiries by 28% over six months.

Not every job produces a neat percentage. Do not invent one. Scope is also evidence: team size, customer volume, budget, geography, systems used, frequency, or complexity.

The purpose of a number is not decoration. It gives the reader a scale.

## 3. Where are you, and when can you start?

International candidates often omit location because they worry it will count against them.

The omission usually creates a worse problem. The recruiter now has to guess whether the candidate is in Amsterdam, arriving next month, or applying speculatively from another continent.

One factual line can remove the uncertainty:

> Based in Utrecht; available from 1 August 2026.

> Relocating to Rotterdam in September 2026.

> Based in Berlin; open to relocation to the Netherlands.

You do not need a full street address. A city, region, or relocation statement is enough for the hiring decision.

Availability is similarly useful when it is unusual or relevant. A three-month notice period is not necessarily a rejection. An unexplained three-month delay discovered late in the process is more likely to feel like one.

## 4. Can you legally work in the Netherlands?

This question should be answered carefully, not dramatically.

EU, EEA, and Swiss nationals can work in the Netherlands without a Dutch work permit. Many other candidates require an employer-supported route, although exceptions exist. For highly skilled migrants, only an employer recognised by the IND can submit the relevant application, and salary and other conditions apply. ([Government.nl](https://www.government.nl/faq/foreign-citizens-working-in-the-netherlands/what-permits-do-foreign-workers-need), [IND](https://ind.nl/en/residence-permits/work/highly-skilled-migrant))

Your CV is not an immigration application. It does not need passport numbers, residence-document numbers, or a legal essay.

It may need one accurate sentence:

> Eligible to work in the Netherlands without sponsorship.

> Currently holds a Dutch orientation-year residence permit.

> Requires employer sponsorship for a highly skilled migrant permit.

Only write what is true. If your situation is complicated, keep the CV concise and clarify the details with the recruiter.

The point is not to make sponsorship disappear. It is to prevent the employer from discovering it after investing three interviews.

## 5. What can you actually do in Dutch?

"Basic Dutch" is nearly meaningless.

It could mean knowing twenty words. It could mean handling a simple conversation but not professional writing. "Conversational" is only slightly better.

Use a recognised scale when you can. Europass uses the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages, from A1 to C2, and allows candidates to assess reading, listening, speaking, and writing separately. ([Europass](https://europass.europa.eu/en/how-self-assess-your-language-skills))

For most CVs, a concise version is enough:

> English: C1 professional  
> Dutch: A2, currently taking weekly lessons  
> German: B1 conversational

If the difference between spoken and written ability matters, say so:

> Dutch: B1 conversation, A2 writing

Do not translate an English CV into Dutch merely to look local if you cannot discuss it in Dutch during an interview. If the vacancy and working language are English, a clear English CV is usually more credible than awkward Dutch.

Official Work in NL guidance makes the practical distinction: some jobs are available in English, while learning Dutch expands the range of opportunities. ([Work in NL](https://www.workinnl.nl/en/employment/finding-work/default.aspx))

Your CV should not hide your current level or apologise for it. It should help the employer decide whether the level matches the role.

## 6. What does your education mean here?

Foreign degrees create two common mistakes.

The first is leaving the original qualification unexplained:

> B.Tech (CSE)

A Dutch reader may understand it, but why make them decode an abbreviation?

Try:

> Bachelor of Technology in Computer Science and Engineering (four-year degree)

The second mistake is inventing a Dutch equivalence:

> Equivalent to Dutch HBO Bachelor

Do not claim an official equivalence unless you have a basis for it.

Nuffic and SBB provide credential evaluations through IDW. Nuffic is explicit that an evaluation compares a foreign diploma with a Dutch level, but the result is not legally binding and employers still decide whom to hire. ([Nuffic](https://www.nuffic.nl/en/study-and-work-in-the-netherlands/applying-for-a-credential-evaluation))

Most unregulated jobs do not require every foreign degree to be formally evaluated. Regulated professions are different and may require recognition or registration. ([Business.gov.nl](https://business.gov.nl/regulations/professional-qualifications/))

For the CV, translate the degree name into plain English, keep the original institution, add the country, and include an official Dutch comparison only when you actually have one.

## 7. Can the document be read without effort?

Design is useful when it creates hierarchy.

It is harmful when it competes with the content.

The recruiter needs to distinguish job title, employer, dates, location, and achievements without learning your visual system. Parsing software also has concrete limits. Greenhouse, for example, lists graphics, image-only resumes, tables, headers and footers, text boxes, columns, and inconsistent sections among the formats that can prevent information from being imported correctly. ([Greenhouse Support](https://support.greenhouse.io/hc/en-us/articles/200989175-Unsuccessful-resume-parse))

This does not mean every CV must look identical. It means visual decisions should have a job.

Use:

- readable type;
- consistent dates;
- clear section headings;
- reverse-chronological experience;
- enough white space to separate ideas;
- real text rather than screenshots;
- one or two pages when the experience justifies it.

Avoid skill bars that claim you are "85% leadership." Avoid icons that replace words such as phone or email. Avoid a two-column puzzle in which dates, employers, and descriptions no longer line up.

The European Commission's Europass guidance arrives at the same basic principles: tailor the document to the vacancy, focus on relevant facts, use clear language, and list recent experience first. ([Europass](https://europass.europa.eu/en/create-europass-cv))

The best design does not make the CV look expensive. It makes the candidate look easy to understand.

## The uncertainty test

Before sending your next application, read only the first half of your first page.

Then answer these questions:

- What role is this person targeting?
- What are they demonstrably good at?
- Where are they based?
- What languages can they use at work?
- Is their work-authorisation situation clear when relevant?
- Can I understand their most recent role without knowing their previous country's job-title system?
- Is there at least one piece of evidence, rather than a list of traits?

If the answers are not visible, adding more adjectives will not help.

Remove uncertainty first.

While building WerkCV, I turned this framework into a practical [Dutch CV checker for the Netherlands](https://werkcv.nl/en/dutch-cv-checker). You can use it to inspect an existing CV for local structure, readability, and missing context. But the underlying test does not require software: every sentence should either help the reader classify you, trust you, or understand your fit.

Everything else is taking up space.

## The real meaning of a Dutch CV

There is no secret Dutch format that guarantees an interview.

A Dutch CV is not a British CV with a photograph, an American résumé with a different spelling, or a Europass document with more colour.

It is a document adapted to a specific decision.

The employer is deciding whether your experience is relevant, whether the practical conditions work, and whether a conversation is worth the time. International applicants lose when their CV hides the information needed for that decision behind unfamiliar titles, vague language, excessive detail, or silence.

Your experience does not become less valuable when it crosses a border.

But its meaning can become less obvious.

The job of the CV is to restore that meaning.
